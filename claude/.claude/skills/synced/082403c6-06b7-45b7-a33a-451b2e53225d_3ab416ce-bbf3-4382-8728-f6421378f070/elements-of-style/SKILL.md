# The Elements of Style — a writing style

Use this when writing or revising prose. Write plain, clear, vigorous English, after Strunk and White's *The Elements of Style*. Aim for cleanliness, accuracy, and brevity. The governing rule: a sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences. Make every word tell.

## Principles of composition

**Omit needless words.** Cut padding — *the fact that*, *in order to*, *there is no doubt that*, *the question as to whether*. Prefer *because* to *owing to the fact that*, *whether* to *the question whether*, *if* to *in the event that*. Drop superfluous *who is* and *which was*: write "his brother, a member of the firm," not "his brother, who is a member of the firm." If a sentence keeps its meaning when a word is removed, remove the word.

**Use the active voice.** It is more direct and vigorous than the passive: "I shall always remember my first visit," not "My first visit will always be remembered by me." Keep the passive only when the receiver of the action matters more than the actor.

**Put statements in positive form.** Make definite assertions. Use *not* for denial or antithesis, never as a means of evasion. Prefer *forgot* to *did not remember*, *ignored* to *did not pay attention to*, *distrusted* to *did not have much confidence in*.

**Use definite, specific, concrete language.** Prefer the specific to the general, the concrete to the abstract, the particular to the vague. Detail persuades where generality drifts.

**Place the emphatic words at the end.** The end of a sentence is the position of prominence; put the new or important element there. The same principle governs the sentences of a paragraph and the paragraphs of a piece.

**Express coordinate ideas in similar form.** Parallel construction lets the reader recognize likeness of content through likeness of form.

**Keep related words together.** Keep the subject near its verb, modifiers next to what they modify, a relative pronoun next to its antecedent. Word order is the chief means of showing relationship.

**Avoid a succession of loose sentences.** Don't string clause after clause with *and*, *but*, *which*, *while*. Vary the structure so the syntax mirrors the real relations of the thought.

**Make the paragraph the unit of composition.** One topic to a paragraph. As a rule, open with a topic sentence and close in keeping with it; don't trail off into digression.

**Choose a suitable design and hold to it.** Gauge the size of the task and shape the piece to fit — a note needs no scaffolding, a long argument does. Decide the order, then keep to it.

## An approach to style

These are reminders, not rules — the way good prose tends to read.

- **Stay in the background.** Draw attention to the sense and substance, not to the mood and temper of the writer. To achieve style, begin by affecting none.
- **Write naturally, with nouns and verbs.** Use words that come readily and honestly, and lean on nouns and verbs, not adjectives and adverbs, for force and color. No adjective can rescue a weak noun.
- **Revise.** Treat the first version as a draft. Cut, transpose, and tighten without sentiment.
- **Don't overwrite.** Rich, ornate prose tires the reader. Resist the lure of the extra clause and the sweet phrase; reread and delete the excess.
- **Don't overstate.** A single careless superlative makes the reader doubt everything around it. Claim only what you can stand behind.
- **Avoid qualifiers.** *Rather, very, little, pretty* leech the blood from the words around them. Where emphasis is wanted, choose a word strong in itself.
- **Don't affect a breezy manner.** Windy, jaunty, self-pleased prose calls attention to the writer and away from the work. Report; don't perform.
- **Don't explain too much.** Be sparing with adverbs after *said* and *replied*; let the words themselves carry the manner. Trust the reader.
- **Avoid awkward adverbs.** Don't manufacture *-ly* words the ear rejects (*tiredly*, *thusly*, *overly*); prefer *wearily*, *thus*, *over*. Words seldom spoken seldom belong on the page.
- **Avoid fancy words.** Prefer the short, plain, Anglo-Saxon word to the elaborate or pretentious one — the ten-cent word over the twenty-dollar one — unless the longer word is genuinely the right one. Let the ear decide.
- **Be clear.** Clarity comes first. Muddiness is a failure of the writer, not the reader; when a passage turns murky, recast it.
- **Don't inject opinion.** Leave out unneeded opinion; let the facts and the writing make the case.
- **Use figures of speech sparingly.** A good simile illuminates; a crowd of them distracts. One at a time, and not too often.
- **Don't take shortcuts at the cost of clarity.** Abbreviation, jargon, and cleverness aren't worth a confused reader.
- **Prefer the standard to the offbeat.** Favor conventional usage and orthodox spelling over the faddish and eccentric; novelty for its own sake wears badly.

## A few points of usage
- *That* defines (restrictive, no comma); *which* adds nonessential information (set off by commas).
- *Fewer* for number, *less* for quantity. *Compare to* for resemblance, *compare with* for difference.
- Don't stretch *while* to mean *and*, *but*, or *although*; reserve it for *during the time that*.
- Choose *who* and *whom* by grammatical role, not by guesswork.

## Format
Default to prose. Reserve lists, headings, and other scaffolding for material that is genuinely enumerable or that the reader must scan; don't fragment continuous thought into bullets. The style governs prose — leave code, data tables, and step-by-step instructions formatted as their content requires.
