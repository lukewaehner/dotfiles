# Buyer branch: financial sponsor

The fund competes with nobody directly. Its antitrust exposure lives one layer down, in the
portfolio — often dozens of holdings, many private, some minority, with no consolidated filing to
read. The hard work here is **discovering the competitive entity before screening it**, and the
deal-relevant overlap is frequently a portfolio company the announcement never mentions. Do not skip
enumeration and jump to the obvious overlap.

**This branch governs every buyer-side element of the memo, not just Section B.** Section C's
Verbatim Competitor Disclosures table, Geographic Revenue Comparison table, and Market Structure
table all have a "buyer-side" slot — on a sponsor deal that slot is filled by the relevant portfolio
company, never by the fund. The fund itself is the correct subject in exactly three places anywhere in
the memo: the buyer-classification evidence (Section B), the firm profile (AUM, fund, thesis), and the
fund's own prior antitrust/HSR-compliance history as a filer. Everywhere else — every overlap
sentence, every competitor-disclosure row, every geographic-revenue row — name the specific portfolio
company. If a draft sentence has the fund's name as its subject next to "overlap," "competes," or
"revenue by geography," that sentence is almost certainly wrong and needs the portfolio company
substituted in.

## Mapping the sponsor

- **Enumerate the current portfolio — majority AND minority stakes.** Sources in order of
  reliability: portfolio-company EDGAR filings (13D/G, S-1s naming the sponsor, proxy ownership
  tables), the sponsor's own website portfolio page, 13F where applicable, then reputable press and
  PE-database coverage found via web search. Mark each holding verified (EDGAR / sponsor disclosure)
  or reported-only (press). Note the stake type. **Minority stakes are flagged, never dropped** —
  regulators reach them through interlock, information-sharing and partial-ownership theories, and a
  dropped minority stake is a missed overlap that the memo will never recover.
- **Historical portfolio and exits in the target's sector** — past platforms, add-on chains, exit
  outcomes. A realized position in the target's industry is evidence about the pattern even though
  the sponsor no longer owns it.
- **Sponsor regulatory history** — prior FTC / DOJ / EC / CMA reviews, second requests, consent
  decrees, required divestitures, abandoned deals, live behavioural remedies. Search agency press
  releases and trade press directly, and state the result as an explicit positive or negative
  finding. A sponsor with a consent decree in this sector faces a different review than a first-timer.
- **Investment thesis and platform vs. add-on characterisation.** Is the target a new platform, or an
  add-on to a platform the sponsor already owns? An add-on into the same market is the
  highest-scrutiny configuration there is on the sponsor side, because the overlap is by design.

**Deliverable — Portfolio Company Analysis table:** portfolio company | stake (majority / minority /
realized) | sector & product lines | verified via (cited) | relevance to target.

**Enumerate the full current portfolio in this table, not only the holdings that look relevant.**
Pull the sponsor's complete portfolio-page listing and include every current holding as a row, with
"relevance to target" stating "None — [sector]" for the large majority that don't overlap. A short
list of only the holdings a web search happened to surface reads as cherry-picked even when the
underlying screening was thorough — a complete list with mostly "None" rows is what makes the two or
three genuine overlaps credible and auditable to a reader who wants to check the work.

## Screening the portfolio

Cross every footprint entry against the target's product/service lines and geographies:

- Classify each as direct horizontal / vertical (specify upstream or downstream) /
  adjacent-complementary / potential future competition / none.
- For private portfolio companies with no filings, classify from sponsor descriptions and press, cite
  them, and mark the classification lower-confidence. Lower confidence stated is worth more than
  false precision.
- **Roll-up seriality**: count the sponsor's acquisitions in this sector over the past 5–10 years and
  list them. Agencies increasingly treat serial small acquisitions as cumulative concentration even
  where each individually cleared its thresholds, so a long add-on chain is a risk factor independent
  of this deal's standalone overlap. Say so with the count and the list, not as a generality.

**Deliverable — Overlap Screen table:** portfolio company | overlap type | overlapping target line |
geography | evidence citation.

## Sponsor-specific theories of harm

Carry these into the competitive-effects table alongside the standard theories:

- **Roll-up seriality** — the acquisition pattern read as cumulative concentration.
- **Cross-portfolio bundling / cross-selling** — portfolio companies packaging offerings together or
  steering customers between them.
- **Data advantage** — combined datasets across portfolio companies and the target, and whether the
  combination creates a data asset no rival can replicate.
- **Common ownership and interlocks** — minority stakes in the target's competitors, and board seats
  or information rights that come with them.
- **Vertical alignment across the portfolio** — portfolio companies that supply to or buy from the
  target, creating foreclosure incentives no single company's filings reveal because no single
  company sees the whole picture.

## What the divestiture analysis looks like on a sponsor deal

Remedy candidates on a sponsor deal are frequently a *portfolio company* rather than a piece of the
target — the sponsor divests the overlapping holding it already owns, which is often cleaner than
carving up the newly acquired business. Name the specific holding, the concern it addresses and
whether it is separable, and note that a sponsor's willingness to sell an existing position is
usually higher than a strategic's willingness to break up an acquisition it just paid for.

## The sponsor as regulatory applicant — a different question from the sponsor as portfolio owner

Everything above treats the sponsor as an **owner of competing assets**, which is the right frame for
antitrust. On a **sector-gated deal it is the wrong frame**, and running only the portfolio screen
will produce a memo that answers a question the reviewing regulator is not asking.

A change-of-control review — a Form A, a qualifying-holding notification, a significant-interest
application — assesses **the applicant itself**: who the ultimate controlling persons are, whether
they are fit and proper, what they intend to do with the regulated entity, and how they will
capitalise it. The portfolio is nearly irrelevant to that. The fund structure, the track record, and
the business plan are the review.

Return these alongside the portfolio map whenever a sector regulator is on the critical path:

- **Has this sponsor ever been an approved control person of a regulated entity of this type** — in
  this jurisdiction or any other? A repeat applicant has a mapped controlling-person chain, biographical
  affidavits on file and prior commitments on record; a first-timer builds all of it from scratch. This
  single fact moves the archetype classification more than anything else about the firm.
- **How many tiers deep is the ownership chain, and does it cross borders?** Fund, parallel funds, GP,
  management company, co-investors, offshore holding entities. Regulators require every ultimate
  controlling person identified, and building that chart to a supervisor's satisfaction is a project
  with real duration for a large multi-fund manager.
- **Is there acquisition leverage, and where does it sit relative to the regulated entity?** Debt
  above the regulated entity is a different conversation from debt at it.
- **Are there affiliated arrangements** — an in-house investment manager, an affiliated reinsurer, a
  services agreement — that the regulator will price as related-party risk?
- **What does the sponsor's stated thesis imply about capital?** A thesis built on margin expansion
  and distributions reads differently to a prudential regulator than one built on growth investment,
  and the regulator will ask.

**Expect the review to end in negotiated commitments rather than refusal**, and say so. Capital
maintenance agreements, dividend restrictions for a stated term, advance-notice undertakings on
investment or reinsurance policy, board-composition requirements, minimum capital floors above the
statutory level. This matters for the memo because **negotiating commitments is calendar**: it is the
mechanism by which a sponsor's application runs long without anyone objecting to the deal, and naming
it is the difference between "PE deals take longer" and an explanation a reader can act on.

**And look hard for continuity.** Where an existing controlling shareholder rolls equity and remains
a control person, or founders and management stay, the regulator is being asked to approve an
addition to an ownership chain it has already vetted rather than an unfamiliar one — and the "who
will actually run this" limb is answered before it is asked. This is the strongest timing mitigant
available on a sponsor deal. State it against the specific approvals it helps, and be honest that it
helps most where the incumbent holder is already approved and least where a supervisor assesses the
new applicant on its own merits.
