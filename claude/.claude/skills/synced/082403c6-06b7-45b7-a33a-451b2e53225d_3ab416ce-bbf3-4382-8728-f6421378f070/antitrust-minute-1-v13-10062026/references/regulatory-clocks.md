# Regulatory clocks — per-regime change-of-control mechanics

The encoded domain knowledge behind Section E's **Approval Clock Build**. Read during the gather
window. Every entry answers the same five questions, because those are the five the build table needs:

1. **Statutory clock** — what the law says.
2. **What starts it** — almost never the date of filing.
3. **Interruption mechanics** — how the clock stops, and who controls that.
4. **Observed practical range** — what deals actually take.
5. **Acquirer attributes that lengthen it** — the archetype overlay.

**Use these as the default and verify live.** These are durable structural facts, not current
disclosures; statutory periods change, supervisory postures move, and a regulator's practical range
in 2028 is not necessarily its range today. Agent E's job is to confirm each critical-path row
against the regulator's own guidance and recent law-firm commentary. What this file buys you is that
you are verifying a specific number rather than discovering the existence of a mechanism.

## The general shape

Nearly every change-of-control regime has the same architecture, and recognising it is worth more
than memorising any single number:

**A short headline period, running from a complete filing, with a deficiency mechanism that makes
completeness the elastic variable.** The statute promises sixty days; the applicant spends four
months getting to a filing the regulator agrees is complete. The gap between those two facts is where
deal timelines actually live, and it is almost never disclosed.

Three structural consequences follow, and they should shape every row of the build:

- **Pre-filing engagement is real time and is not clocked.** Most prudential supervisors expect
  informal engagement before a formal notification, and applicants who skip it get deficiency
  letters instead. Budget it.
- **Deficiency letters reset practical completeness, not always the statutory clock.** Whether the
  clock formally stops varies by regime; whether the calendar moves does not.
- **The applicant controls filing quality; the regulator controls everything after.** This is why
  acquirer archetype predicts duration — a repeat applicant with a mapped controlling-person chain
  and prior commitments on file files complete the first time.

## Insurance — US state departments of insurance (Form A)

**The regime.** State enactments of the NAIC Insurance Holding Company System Regulatory Act (Model
#440). Approval is required from the **domiciliary** regulator of each insurer being acquired — not
from every state the insurer writes in. This distinction routinely halves or doubles an estimate:
count domiciles, not licences.

**Trigger.** A rebuttable presumption of control at **10%** of voting securities, direct or indirect.
Below 10%, or where control is genuinely absent, a **disclaimer of control** filing is the
alternative — faster, but realistically available only where the acquirer takes no board
representation and no governance rights, which a control buyout does not.

**Statutory clock.** Typically the commissioner must act within roughly **60 days of a completed
filing**; some states run 30 days to a hearing decision after a hearing concludes. Public hearings
are **mandatory in some states and discretionary in others** — where mandatory, the hearing is
usually noticed within ~30 days of completeness with a decision within ~30 days after.

**What starts it.** The commissioner's determination that the Form A is **complete**. Not the
submission date.

**What actually drives duration** — the filing contents, which is where a generalist read goes wrong:

- **Identification of every ultimate controlling person up the chain.** For a fund structure with
  multiple GP/LP tiers, offshore holding entities and co-investors, building this chart to a
  regulator's satisfaction is a project.
- **NAIC Form 11 biographical affidavits with independent third-party background reports** for each
  director and officer of the applicant and of each ultimate controlling person. These have real lead
  times, they expire, and one missing affidavit holds the filing incomplete.
- **A five-year business plan with pro forma financials** for the insurer under new ownership.
- **Source-of-funds disclosure**, including any acquisition debt and where it sits relative to the
  insurer.

**Observed practical range.** Four to six months from filing is a reasonable default for a
straightforward applicant; longer where a hearing is mandatory, where multiple domiciles are involved
with differing postures, or where the acquirer archetype triggers the overlay below. Deals whose
*entire* perimeter is one or two US domiciles have closed inside 150–180 days announce-to-close;
multi-domicile deals cluster meaningfully higher.

**Lengtheners:** first-time owner of a licensed carrier; complex or offshore ownership chain;
acquisition leverage; planned dividend or capital extraction; redomestication plans; affiliated
service or investment-management arrangements; any adverse regulatory history among the named
individuals.

**Where to verify live.** State DOI websites publish Form A instructions, filing checklists, hearing
calendars and — importantly — **approval orders**, which give you real per-approval day counts for
the precedent table.

## Insurance — Form E, the state competition filing

**This is the competition filing inside the insurance regime, and it should be addressed
affirmatively rather than omitted.** Form E pre-acquisition notification is required where the
acquirer and the target **write the same line of business in the same state** above statutory
thresholds, and carries a **30-day waiting period** during which the commissioner may review
competitive effect.

Two consequences for the memo:

- Where the acquirer bears **no** insurance risk — a software sponsor, an asset manager with no
  carrier — Form E is **inapplicable**, and saying so is a positive finding worth one sentence. It
  tells the reader the competition limb of the state review is empty before they wonder about it.
- Where both parties do write, Form E is a genuine second competition review running alongside HSR on
  a different substantive standard, and it belongs in the Approval Clock Build as its own row.

Note also that Form A itself carries a competition limb: the commissioner weighs, among other
factors, the acquirer's financial strength, its plans for the insurer, and **any anticompetitive
effect of the acquisition of control**. On a no-overlap deal that limb is easy — but say that it
exists and that it is satisfied, rather than letting the reader assume the competition analysis
stopped at HSR.

## Insurance — the private-equity-ownership overlay

**The single most commonly missed timing input on a sponsor acquisition of a carrier.** Following the
NAIC's Macroprudential (E) Working Group work on regulatory considerations for private-equity-owned
insurers (adopted 2022 and extended since), a first-time private-equity owner of a licensed carrier
draws a category of question a strategic acquirer does not:

- Affiliated investment-management arrangements and the fees flowing to the sponsor's own affiliates.
- Capital extraction and dividend plans, and whether the insurer will be levered to fund them.
- Reserve adequacy and asset-quality expectations under new ownership, particularly where the
  investment policy is expected to change.
- Complex, opaque or offshore ownership structures, and the identification of the real controlling
  persons behind them.
- Reliance on offshore reinsurance or affiliated reinsurance structures.

**These are commonly resolved through negotiated commitments rather than refusal** — capital
maintenance agreements, dividend restrictions for a stated term, advance-notice undertakings on
investment-policy or reinsurance changes, board-composition requirements, and minimum
capital/RBC floors above the statutory level.

**Negotiating those commitments is calendar.** This is why a private-equity Form A runs long even
when nobody objects to the deal on its merits, and it is the mechanism a memo should name when it
assigns a sponsor acquirer a slower archetype. The right sentence is not "PE deals take longer" — it
is "the review is a negotiation over commitments, and negotiations have a duration that no statutory
clock describes."

## Insurance — state captive insurance regimes (change of control)

**Added after a real gap: a T+0 read defaulted an ancillary licensed captive subsidiary to the general
Form A entry above, when Tennessee in fact carves captives out of that regime entirely.** Confirm which
regime actually applies before building this row — it is the single most common way a small ancillary
licensed entity gets mis-timed in an otherwise clean distribution or diversified-holdco deal.

**The regime is usually a carve-out from Form A, not a subset of it — check this first.** Most states
regulate captive insurers (single-parent, protected-cell/segregated-cell, risk-retention-group and
similar non-market-facing structures) under a **separate captive insurance act and administrative
chapter**, and that chapter routinely exempts captives from the general Insurance Holding Company System
Act outright. Tennessee is the calibrated example: Tenn. Code Ann. §56-13-116 states that "no provisions
of this title, other than those contained in this chapter... shall apply to captive insurance companies"
— a Tennessee captive is not subject to the standard 60-day-hearing Form A machinery above at all. Do
not assume a captive subsidiary triggers the general Form A analysis; confirm the state's own captive
act's carve-out language first.

**What actually governs change of control is typically one rule, not a statute, and it is usually
unclocked.** Where a captive-specific change-of-control provision exists, expect language close to
Tennessee Rule 0780-01-41-.11: prior written approval of the commissioner is required, who considers
"all of the facts and circumstances... as well as the criteria for establishment of a company" — the
same substantive bar as a new captive license application, with **no statutory clock stated**. Check two
things alongside it: whether the state gives its commissioner express discretion to waive or modify
public notice and hearing requirements for captive transactions (Tennessee: §56-13-107(g)), and whether
non-material changes require only post-hoc notice rather than prior approval (Tennessee: Rule
0780-01-41-.12, a 60-day post-hoc notice window). Where both exist, the captive review is real and
substantive — not a rubber stamp — but it is staffed and run as a license-application review, not a
contested Form A hearing, and is capable of moving materially faster than the parent's own general
insurance-holding-company posture would suggest.

**Benchmark duration off the state's own new-captive-license processing time, not off Form A.** A
captive division typically publishes (or can be asked for) its average time from a complete new-license
application to approval — Tennessee's Captive Insurance Section runs roughly 30 days. A change-of-control
filing is narrower than a new license (the underwriting program, actuarial certification and capital
structure of the existing captive do not change — only who stands behind it), so that new-license
benchmark is a reasonable **floor**, not a ceiling, for a well-prepared change-of-control filing. The
realistic band for a straightforward captive COC filing is **30–90 days from a complete filing**.

**Where the applicant is genuinely unfamiliar to the captive regulator, use the state's own
hearing-based Form A precedent — for a traditional carrier, not a captive — as the outside-case ceiling,
not the base case.** Discretion to waive notice-and-hearing is exactly where an unfamiliar or complex
applicant can slip into the full track: if the commissioner elects not to exercise a waiver provision,
the captive filing runs the same completeness-driven timeline as a standard Form A. Anchor that outside
case to a real precedent from the same department — e.g., Tennessee's Centene/WellCare Health Insurance
of Tennessee Form A (filed May 2019, deemed complete ~84 days later, hearing held, order entered ~120
days after filing) — and label it explicitly as the ceiling for a large, contested review, not the
expected case for a small single-cell captive with an unchanged business plan, which is the paradigm
case the waiver exists for.

**Biographical/source-of-funds requirements attach to whoever the ultimate controlling persons actually
are — count them, don't assume a fund-scale exercise by default.** The captive filing still requires
biographical affidavits and source-of-funds disclosure for every officer, director and owner of the
applicant and, by extension, every ultimate controlling person above it. Where the acquirer is a single
family office or another simple ownership structure (a handful of named principals) rather than a
multi-tier PE fund, this exercise is structurally faster than the "map every GP/LP/co-investor" project
the Form A entry above describes for a fund structure — say so explicitly as a mitigant, while noting it
does not offset a captive regulator's lack of any prior file on a genuinely first-time applicant.

**Lengtheners specific to the captive line:** the commissioner declining to exercise a notice/hearing
waiver; a captive whose business plan or underwriting program is changing alongside the ownership change
(this converts a change-of-control review into something closer to a new-license review); an applicant
with no prior regulatory file anywhere the captive division can draw from; a multi-tier or offshore
ownership chain even where the operating acquirer itself is simple.

**Where to verify live.** State captive-insurance divisions (often a distinct unit from the general
Department of Insurance, sometimes with its own site and staff) publish the captive act text, the
change-of-control/merger rule if one exists, and typical new-license processing times — read the captive
division's own guidance rather than the general Form A instructions, since the two are frequently
administered on different timelines within the same department.

## Insurance — non-US prudential supervisors

| Supervisor | Threshold | Statutory clock | Clock start | Interruption | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **UK PRA** (insurers) | 10% controller; further approvals crossing 20% / 30% / 50% | **60 working days** | Acknowledgement of a **complete** s.178 notification | Up to **30 working days** where the applicant is based outside the UK; shorter otherwise | Pre-notification engagement is expected and unclocked. Controller-chain mapping for a fund structure is the usual source of incompleteness. |
| **UK FCA** (intermediaries, solo-regulated) | Single threshold at **20%** | 60 working days | As above | As above | Runs in parallel with PRA where a group holds both insurers and intermediaries. Appointed representatives generally do **not** require pre-approval — the principal notifies. |
| **National Bank of Belgium** (Solvency II qualifying holding) | 10%, then 20% / 30% / 50% | **82 working days** (EEA-based acquirer) / **92 working days** (non-EEA acquirer) | Acknowledgement of a complete notification | Information requests suspend | The non-EEA extension is automatic for a US acquirer and is frequently the single longest clocked review in a European insurance deal. |
| **Central Bank of Ireland** — insurers | Qualifying holding, Solvency II standard | Solvency II assessment period | Complete notification | Information requests suspend | — |
| **Central Bank of Ireland** — intermediaries | — | **No pre-approval requirement** | — | — | **Notification without unreasonable delay only.** A real accelerant where a group's Irish presence is intermediary-only; check which it is before assuming a clocked review. |
| **Cayman Islands Monetary Authority** | Change in ownership/control of a licensee | No fixed statutory decision period disclosed | Filing | — | Fit-and-proper assessment of any person acquiring control or ownership. Usually not the binding constraint. |
| **Canada — OSFI + Minister of Finance** | Significant interest / control under the Insurance Companies Act | **No statutory shot clock on the ministerial step** | OSFI review, then ministerial decision | Ministerial discretion | OSFI reviews and recommends; the **Minister of Finance approves**. Sequenced, not parallel — and unclocked. Treat as a structural long pole on any Canadian carrier. Screen separately for Investment Canada Act applicability. |
| **Bermuda Monetary Authority** | Shareholder controller thresholds | Notice-based | Filing | — | Generally tractable; confirm whether approval or notification. |
| **BaFin / other EEA supervisors** | Solvency II qualifying holding | 60 working days, +30 for non-EEA applicants | Acknowledgement of complete notification | Information requests suspend | The Solvency II baseline; Belgium's longer periods are a national elaboration. |
| **MAS / APRA / HKIA** | Varies | Varies | — | — | Research live where in perimeter. |

**Two cross-cutting points that matter more than any single row.** First, **overseas branches
generally do not require pre-approval for a change of control** — the home-state procedure applies
and the branch notifies afterward. A group operating through branches rather than subsidiaries has a
materially shorter perimeter than its jurisdiction count suggests, so count *authorised entities*,
not countries of operation. Second, a passporting insurer serving twenty EEA states through one
authorisation needs approval from **its home supervisor only**, not from twenty. Getting this wrong
in either direction is the fastest way to produce a badly wrong timeline.

## Financial-services ancillary regimes — FINRA and RIA/Investment Advisers Act

**Read this on every deal, regardless of sector — not only insurance deals.** It exists because a
target's own 10-K "Regulation" section routinely discloses regulator relationships that have nothing
to do with the deal's primary sector thesis, sitting in the same paragraph or two as the language
Agent E is already reading for the sector-specific perimeter, and these get missed precisely because
the brief was scoped to the sector everyone already assumed mattered. The BWIN/Baldwin deal is the
worked example this entry was built from: an insurance-distribution target's own FY25 10-K disclosed,
in the same "Regulation" section as its state insurance-licensing language, that its portfolio
included several SEC-registered investment advisers and a FINRA-registered limited-purpose
broker-dealer — a fact available from a primary source filed roughly seven months before the deal was
announced, surfaced only via a same-week broker note rather than the initial gather pass. That is a
process gap to close, not a data-availability one, and this entry exists so it does not recur.

**FINRA broker-dealer membership.** If the target's own disclosure names any broker-dealer
subsidiary registered with FINRA — including a "limited purpose" broker-dealer, a common structure
for firms whose BD registration exists mainly to handle M&A/private-placement fee flows rather than
public securities dealing — a change of control transferring 25% or more of that entity's ownership
(or the power to elect a majority of its directors) requires **prior FINRA approval via a Continuing
Membership Application under FINRA Rule 1017**, before that entity's ownership can change hands.
1. **Statutory clock** — no fixed statutory period; FINRA's own public guidance describes CMA review
   as commonly resolving in roughly 30 days once the application is deemed complete, but market
   practice on relatively simple ownership-change CMAs runs closer to 4-4.5 months net of pre-filing
   preparation and supplemental-information cycles.
2. **What starts it** — the filing date of a complete CMA, not the transaction's signing date;
   pre-filing engagement with FINRA staff is customary and itself takes calendar time to arrange.
3. **Interruption mechanics** — FINRA staff can request additional information, which restarts or
   pauses the completeness clock; unlike a state insurance Form A, there is typically no public
   docket to track this against.
4. **Observed practical range** — treat as its own row in Section E.2's Approval Clock Build, not
   folded into or averaged with the primary sector's clock; verify against recent Rule 1017 CMA
   filings or law-firm commentary where available, and flag as an assumption where it isn't.
5. **Acquirer attributes that lengthen it** — a financial or non-industry-strategic acquirer with no
   prior FINRA-member ownership draws the same "first-time regulated owner" scrutiny this file's PE
   overlay describes for insurance regulators; a buyer with an existing FINRA-member affiliate may
   move faster.

**SEC-registered investment adviser (RIA) status.** A target that discloses one or more RIA
subsidiaries is subject to the Investment Advisers Act of 1940's assignment rule (Section
205(a)(2), and state-law analogues for state-registered advisers): a change of control is typically
treated in the industry as an assignment of each advisory contract, which by statute may not occur
without client consent. In practice this runs as a **negative/deemed-consent notice-and-comment
process to each advisory client** (a stated notice period, silence treated as consent, an
affirmative-consent carve-out where the underlying advisory agreement requires it) rather than a
regulator's prior approval — **this is a client-facing, AUM-retention covenant risk, not a
government closing-condition clock**, and should be described that way rather than equated to a
Form A or a FINRA CMA. A post-closing Form ADV amendment is the standard follow-on filing once the
change of control is effective; it documents the change rather than gating it.

**Foreign regulatory footprint.** Any country the target's own disclosure names as an operating
jurisdiction is a prompt to identify the specific licensed entity and its regulator before assuming
a purely domestic regulatory architecture — a target can disclose "we operate in [country]" as one
sentence in the Regulation section while the actual gating question (is there a locally licensed
entity, and does change of control there require prior consent or mere notification) needs its own
research pass. Two frequent examples, not an exhaustive list: a UK insurance-distribution operation
is very likely FCA-authorised (arranging or advising on insurance is a regulated activity under
FSMA), which commonly points to a Section 178 FSMA change-of-control notification; a Bermuda
operation runs through the BMA if there is a licensed entity there rather than only a placement or
service office. Where the specific entity or regulator cannot be confirmed from the 10-K alone, say
so as an open item rather than assuming the footprint is immaterial because it isn't the deal's
headline sector.

## Health care — state transaction-notice laws and state health licensure

**Added after a real gap: on a multi-state home-infusion target the first read named the gate "state pharmacy
licensing", recorded the real state list as "unfiled", and never tested the state transaction-notice statutes.**
The full reference, with the reference table of states, the perimeter test and the output template, is in
`references/state-healthcare-regimes.md`. The load-bearing mechanics are:

- **Three layers.** (A) State transaction-notice and review laws, triggered by revenue, asset or presence
  thresholds. (B) Licensure and change of ownership: pharmacy, home infusion, home health, certificate of need.
  (C) Federal program enrollment and accreditation, usually notification only.
- **Threshold-triggered, so the 10-K does not list them.** Test the statute's revenue or asset measure at the
  right level (target, acquirer's ultimate parent, sponsor and affiliates where counted). A location count is only a
  proxy.
- **Notice-only, review, or prior approval.** Only prior-approval regimes and long lead times sit on the critical
  path. Long notice lead times (California 90 days, Maine 180 days **[secondary, re-verify]**) set a hard earliest
  date; comprehensive reviews (Oregon up to 180 days, Massachusetts up to about 215 days) set the tail.
- **Clock start is a complete notice, and completeness is elastic.** Oregon's clock can be extended while the
  agency waits for information. State the clock as "from complete notice, before tolling".
- **The agreement may require the filings quickly (for example 30 business days) and at a higher efforts standard
  than antitrust.** Count the deadline with `deal_clock.py bdays`; compute floors and tails with `deal_clock.py
  adddays`.
- **Where to verify live.** Each agency's own page, a current law-firm guide to health care transaction laws
  (laws change yearly), and the agencies' public notice pages for the target's earlier filings.
- **Lengtheners:** a private-equity or other buyer a statute names specifically; a deal that also needs a cost and
  market impact review in a state where the buyer's parent has large in-state revenue; a target with many licensed
  pharmacies in slow states; an information request in a state that tolls the clock.

## Other sectors — stubs

Not built out. Where one of these is on the critical path, Agent E researches it live against the
regulator's own guidance and recent law-firm commentary; the notes here are the load-bearing facts to
anchor that research and to prevent the most common errors.

- **FCC** — the **180-day informal transaction shot clock** is exactly that: informal, and routinely
  **paused** for information requests, refiling, or referral. Treat 180 days as a floor and the pause
  history of comparable transactions as the real signal. Licence transfers and foreign-ownership
  review (Team Telecom / the Committee for the Assessment of Foreign Participation) can run
  alongside.
- **FERC** — Federal Power Act §203 authorisation. Notice-and-comment; contested proceedings extend
  substantially.
- **STB** — major rail combinations run a **statutory schedule measured in many months** with a
  formal evidentiary record; treat as a proceeding, not an approval, and note that antitrust input
  flows through the agency rather than around it.
- **Federal Reserve / OCC / FDIC** — Change in Bank Control Act and Bank Holding Company Act
  approvals; public comment periods, CRA considerations, and control-determination questions for
  fund investors.
- **State public utility commissions** — highly variable, often the longest pole in a utility deal;
  frequently involve settlement negotiations with intervenors that resemble the insurance
  commitments dynamic.
- **CFIUS** — 45-day review, 45-day investigation, possible presidential referral; declaration track
  shorter. Mandatory in defined cases, voluntary otherwise.
- **Gaming, defence (DCSA/DDTC), NRC, DOT/FAA** — research live.
- **State health care transaction laws and health licensure** — now built out above; see
  `references/state-healthcare-regimes.md`.

## Building the table from these entries

For each approval body on the perimeter:

1. **Confirm the entity actually requires approval**, versus notification, versus nothing —
   subsidiary vs. branch vs. appointed representative, domiciliary vs. licensed-only, passported vs.
   locally authorised. **For a US insurance entity specifically, also confirm it isn't a captive (or
   similar carved-out structure) before defaulting to the Form A entry** — check the state's captive
   insurance act for its own carve-out language first; see the captive entry above. **Before finalizing
   the perimeter for any sector, also confirm the target's own "Regulation" section was read in full
   for ancillary regulator categories outside the assumed sector** — FINRA/broker-dealer, RIA, or a
   named foreign footprint — per the "Financial-services ancillary regimes" entry above; these are
   available from the target's own filings on day one and should not surface only via a secondary
   source. This step eliminates more phantom rows than any other, in both directions.
2. **Estimate the filing date.** Merger agreements typically require filings within 20–40 business
   days of signing; where Ex-2.1 is not yet available, use that convention and label it an
   assumption.
3. **Add pre-filing engagement** where the regime expects it and it is unclocked.
4. **Apply the statutory clock from its real start**, plus the interruption where the acquirer's
   domicile or profile makes it likely rather than merely possible.
5. **Apply the archetype overlay** from the SKILL.md table and the PE overlay above.
6. **Derive the approval date, and flag whether the row is on the critical path.**
7. **Take the latest date, add a short closing tail**, and sanity-check the result against the
   precedent set and the parties' own guidance — reconciling, not averaging.

A row you cannot source is stated as unsourced with the assumption made explicit. A build with two
labelled assumptions is far more useful than one that hides them inside a single confident date.
