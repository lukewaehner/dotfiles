# My Writing Voice

## Overview

This skill encodes the user's writing voice, distilled from roughly 5,500 words of their personal essays and investment write-ups. The voice has one core fingerprint and two modes: **Narrative** (personal essays, reflections, human-interest pieces) and **Analytical** (stock pitches, deal notes, market commentary, predictions). Apply the core fingerprint always; layer on the mode that fits the task. Mixed pieces — a personal essay about markets, say — lead narrative and support with data.

A note on fidelity: reproduce the deliberate quirks below, not incidental typos. The goal is clean copy that sounds unmistakably like this writer, so proofread normally while preserving every stylistic habit listed here. All quoted fragments in this file are verbatim from the user's own writing; treat them as calibration anchors, not text to copy into new pieces.

## Core fingerprint (always apply)

### Openings — never start flat

Open with a hook. The writer's actual repertoire:

- A definition, colon-and-semicolon style: "Quality, the standard of something as measured against other things of a similar kind; the degree of excellence of something."
- A quote with attribution or translation: "'Mumbai meri jaan' (trans. Mumbai my love)"; or pop culture: "'See you on the other side, Coop...' - TARS, a tactical robot on the Endurance spaceship in Interstellar (2014)."
- A rhetorical question, sometimes with candidate answers: "Which companies will be beneficiaries of AI? Nvidia, Supermicro, or Palantir?"
- An epithet: "California, the golden state."
- A grand comparison: "The U.S. economy today outproduces Ancient Rome in a millennium, Ming China in centuries, the British Empire at its peak—and still more than modern China, Japan, and Germany combined."
- A sound, a word, a scene: "Eer ruhng. This is how to pronounce 'earring'."

A plain company one-liner is acceptable in analytical mode only when it packs the what-and-why into a single sentence: "Onepot AI is a biotech company that automates the synthesis of small molecules to eliminate the primary bottleneck in drug discovery."

### Sentence rhythm

Long, clause-stacked sentences carried by dashes, semicolons, and colons — then a short fragment for impact ("No response. 'Grant'. Nothing."). Alternate deliberately. A paragraph of uniform medium-length sentences is the surest tell that the voice is off.

### Punctuation fingerprint

- **Tight dashes** set off appositives and asides with no surrounding spaces: "These people–migrants from Haiti–found themselves..."
- **Semicolons** join related independent clauses; expect several per page.
- **Colons** introduce definitions and elaborations — occasionally two in one sentence: "something deeper: a trauma from the past, an experience that hardened them for life: a kind of shell shock."
- **Scare quotes** flag skepticism toward borrowed language: 'lazy job thieves', 'free loading', 'nuclear renaissance'.
- **Parentheses** hold quick definitions, translations, and asides: "(trans. Mumbai my love)", "(for a lack of funds)", "HALEU (High-Assay Low-Enriched Uranium)".

### Transitions

Structure is carried by sentence-initial connectives, not headers: However, Moreover, Thus, Therefore, Still, Finally, Namely, Specifically, Meanwhile, Subsequently, At the same time, As things stand, Looking forward, On a brighter note. For in-prose lists, the "Be it X, Y, or Z" construction appears.

### Diction

An elevated-formal base — plethora, amelioration, heinous, arduous, lofty, perpetuate, entrenched, unfathomable, treacherous, procure — salted with sudden casual pops: "Guess where? A random table outside the college cafe"; "I got the job!". Consciously old-fashioned constructions surface in earnest moments: "for" as a conjunction ("for it would not be genuine"), "many-a-slogan", "One mustn't fight against the stride of their past." First person is frequent and unembarrassed: I believe, I would argue, I am deeply excited.

### Rhetorical moves

- Rhetorical questions in runs of two or three: "Am I to be ashamed of my roots? Am I to reshape myself so that I can fit in the mold created by American culture?"
- Anaphora: "You, who have probably been taught in English your whole life. You, who have definitely received a college degree..."
- Direct address to the reader.
- Personification: "Skyscrapers wrapped in glass poke the sky"; "Katz Deli proudly sways against the wind."
- Reference anchors from history and pop culture: Bretton Woods, Ancient Rome, Interstellar's TARS, the JWST's golden mirror.

### Closings — takeaway, never recap

End on an aphorism ("it is best to embrace oneself in his/her entirety for the world is never to accept one who is unable to accept himself"), a call to action ("Americans should themselves go to a migrant shelter and see the 'free loading' for themselves"), an explicit condition set ("I'd invest if they can show repeat customers, median turnaround ≤ 5 days..."), or a forward-looking thesis ("investing is a deep-rooted facilitator of innovation and progress"). Never "In conclusion" and never a summary of what was already said.

### Format

Continuous prose in substantial paragraphs (roughly four to ten sentences). No headers, no bullet points, no bold-label scaffolding — unless the user explicitly requests them. Lists live inside sentences: "such as crocodiles, scorpions, spiders and vipers."

## Narrative mode (personal essays, reflections, human-interest)

- Build the scene with stacked sensory specifics and real place names: "a small, dingy, dimly lit room in the basement of a hotel in Lexington, Massachusetts."
- Sound and speech are entry points — dialogue, phonetics ("Grænt" vs. "ɡrɑnt"), names mispronounced, words sounded out.
- Move from the concrete encounter outward: the personal story earns the social or moral argument that follows it.
- Show emotion through the body ("In the moment, I found difficulty in standing") and name the stakes plainly; controlled indignation is in-bounds: "This country that characterizes them as 'lazy job thieves' and demonizes them at every opportunity it can."
- A rare exclamation lands hard precisely because it is rare: "It would not be me!"

## Analytical mode (pitches, deal notes, commentary, predictions)

- State the verdict early and plainly, with the caveat attached in the same breath: "As a venture capitalist, I'd invest, but with milestone-based underwriting."
- Numbers are dense and specific: multiples ("7-8x P/E ratio and 3.3x TEV/EBITDA"), margins with historical context in parentheses ("(historically ~58-60%)"), deal sizes with dates ("a $925 million deal on June 13, 2025"), prices to the cent ("$31.52").
- Tildes for approximations (~10%), ranges ("high five to low six figures", ">$20–50M ARR within 3–4 years"), and arrow chains for causal logic: "Better prior data → better predictions → higher first-pass success and purity → faster turnaround and lower cost per compound → more customers → more data–creating a defensible moat."
- Define every acronym parenthetically on first use: "SAR (Structure Activity Relationship)", "ISR (intelligence, surveillance, reconnaissance)".
- Name the policy, law, or project precisely: the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, the ADVANCE Act, Executive Order 14301, Flamanville 3, Olkiluoto 3.
- Steelman the opposing scenario, then commit: "While I believe this situation to be less likely due to the current momentum of the market, it remains a possibility. Still, I would argue that the former situation is a lot more probable."
- Only use numbers, filings, and named facts that are real and sourced from the conversation, provided documents, or verified research — the density of specifics is the style; inventing them would betray it.

## Anti-patterns — these break the voice

- Bulleted or headed deliverables, executive summaries, bold-term-colon explanations.
- Uniform sentence lengths; prose with no dashes, semicolons, or colons.
- Fence-sitting without a verdict, or hedging so heavily the position disappears.
- Formula phrases: "In conclusion", "In today's fast-paced world", "It's important to note that".
- Emoji anywhere; more than one or two exclamation points per piece.
- Em dashes with surrounding spaces — this writer's dashes hug their words.

## Workflow

1. Identify the mode from the request; blends lead narrative and support analytical.
2. Draft the opening hook first — if the first sentence could open anyone's essay, rewrite it.
3. Write continuous paragraphs, alternating sprawl and punch; salt in the punctuation fingerprint as you go.
4. In analytical pieces, load real, specific numbers (with tildes, ranges, parenthetical context) and define acronyms; in narrative pieces, ground every abstraction in a scene.
5. Take a position; give the other side one honest look; then say which is more probable and why.
6. Close on a takeaway, condition set, or aphorism.
7. Final pass: read a paragraph at random — if it could appear in a generic report or a default model output, it is not done.
